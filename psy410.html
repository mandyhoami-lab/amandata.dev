Chapter 1: Experimental Psychology and the Scientific Method

Key concepts

Psychology became a science by replacing armchair authority with empiricism — knowledge built from systematic observation. Common sense feels right but is full of traps like hindsight bias ("“I knew it all along”"), which is why psychologists insist on testing ideas instead of trusting gut feelings.

Science has four goals: describe behavior accurately, predict when it will occur, explain why it occurs, and control or apply that knowledge. A good theory serves all four: it organizes known facts, predicts new ones, and gets revised when predictions fail.

The engine of the whole enterprise is the theory → hypothesis → test → revise loop. A hypothesis must be falsifiable — stated precisely enough that evidence could prove it wrong. Vague claims that explain everything explain nothing.

Operational definitions are where abstract ideas meet actual measurement: you define a variable by exactly how you manipulate or measure it (e.g., “anxiety” = score on a 20-item scale). Different operations can produce different results, which is why replication — repeating a study to see if the result holds — is the self-correcting heart of science.

Basic research chases understanding for its own sake; applied research solves practical problems. Both feed each other, and both live or die by parsimony — when two explanations fit the data, prefer the simpler one.

Chapter 2: Research Ethics

Key concepts

Modern ethics codes were written in the shadow of real harm: Nazi medical experiments (→ the Nuremberg Code), the Tuskegee syphilis study, Milgram’s obedience work, and Zimbardo’s prison study. Each showed that “interesting results” never justify hurting people.

The Belmont Report (1979) boiled ethics down to three principles: respect for persons (people choose for themselves → informed consent), beneficence (maximize benefits, minimize harm), and justice (don’t dump all the risk on vulnerable groups).

Informed consent means participants know the purpose, procedures, risks, and their right to quit anytime — before they agree. Deception is allowed only when the study can’t work without it, never about risks, and it must be followed by a full debriefing.

An IRB (Institutional Review Board) reviews every study with human participants in advance, weighing the risk/benefit ratio. Studies at or below “minimal risk” — no more than everyday life — get lighter review.

Confidentiality (you know who said what but promise not to tell) is not the same as anonymity (even you can’t link responses to names). Animal research gets its own oversight (IACUC) and the 3 Rs: replace animals when possible, reduce numbers, refine procedures to minimize suffering.

Chapter 7: The Basics of Experimentation

Key concepts

An experiment has three ingredients: you manipulate an independent variable, measure a dependent variable, and control everything else. That combination — and only that combination — lets you claim cause and effect.

Extraneous variables are anything besides the IV that could affect the DV. Most are just noise, but a confounding variable is worse: it varies systematically with the IV, so you can’t tell which one caused the result. A confounded experiment is uninterpretable — the whole point is lost.

Researchers fight confounds with control techniques: random assignment (every participant has an equal shot at each condition — the gold standard), matching (pairing participants on key traits, then splitting pairs), holding conditions constant, and counterbalancing order.

Random assignment is not random selection. Selection is how you pick people from the population (affects generalizability); assignment is how you place them into conditions (affects internal validity). Quizzes love mixing these up.

Internal validity = did the IV really cause the change? Classic threats: history (outside events), maturation (people change over time), testing (the pretest itself teaches), instrumentation (your measuring tool drifts), regression (extreme scores drift toward the average), selection, and mortality (dropouts).

Two human threats: demand characteristics (participants guess the hypothesis and play along — or sabotage it) and experimenter expectancy (the researcher’s expectations leak into the results). Fixes: single-blind (participants don’t know their condition), double-blind (neither do the experimenters), and placebos to control for expectation itself.

Chapter 8: Between-Subjects Designs

Key concepts

In a between-subjects design, each participant experiences only one condition, and you compare different groups of people. The entire logic rests on one requirement: the groups must be equivalent before the IV is applied — otherwise any difference at the end could be pre-existing.

Random assignment is what creates that equivalence. With enough participants, chance evens out age, motivation, ability — everything, including things you never thought to measure. That’s why it’s the defining feature of a true experiment.

When samples are small, researchers hedge with matching (pair people on the key variable, then split the pairs) or block randomization (randomly assign within blocks so each condition gets equal numbers). Both are backups, not upgrades — random assignment alone is still the ideal.

The payoff of between-subjects designs: no order effects, no carryover, every condition gets a “fresh” participant. The price: you need a lot of people, and individual differences between groups become noise (error variance) that can drown out the IV’s effect.

Watch for differential mortality — if more people quit the boring condition than the fun one, the groups are no longer equivalent, and selection bias creeps back in.

Tip: Between-subjects’ superpower is fresh participants in every condition; its weakness is needing lots of them.

Chapter 9: Basic Between-Subjects Designs

Key concepts

The simplest true experiment is the posttest-only design: randomly assign people to experimental vs. control, apply the IV, then measure once. If random assignment did its job, the single posttest difference is the IV’s effect — clean and immune to testing effects, since there’s no pretest to tip anyone off.

Add a pretest and you get the pretest-posttest design: measure everyone before and after. The pretest lets you verify the groups started equal and measure exactly how much each person changed — but the pretest itself can sensitize participants (testing threat) or interact with the IV.

The Solomon four-group design runs both at once: two groups get the pretest, two don’t; one of each gets the treatment. It’s the only design that isolates whether the pretest itself changed the outcome — thorough, but it needs four times the participants.

A multiple-group design uses one IV with three or more levels (low/medium/high dose). More levels let you spot nonlinear patterns — like performance rising then falling — that two levels would miss. (Multiple groups ≠ factorial: there’s still only one IV.)

Tip: Solomon four-group = the “was it the pretest?” design. If a question asks which design detects testing effects, that’s the one.

Chapter 10: Between-Subjects Factorial Designs

Key concepts

A factorial design manipulates two or more IVs (called factors) in the same study, crossing every level of each factor with every level of the others. A 2×2 has two factors with two levels each, making four cells — four groups of participants.

The payoff: you get each factor’s main effect (its overall effect, averaged across the other factor — read it from the marginal means) AND the interaction (whether one factor’s effect depends on the other’s level). The interaction is usually the interesting finding.

Spot an interaction on a graph by looking for non-parallel lines. Parallel lines = no interaction (effects just add up); lines that cross or fan apart = the effect of one factor changes across levels of the other.

When you find an interaction, don’t stop at the main effects — they can mislead. Break it down with simple main effects: the effect of one factor at each single level of the other factor.

A mixed factorial design blends the two worlds: one factor is between-subjects, the other is within-subjects.

Tip: The classic quiz move is spotting an interaction — look for non-parallel lines, or one cell that breaks the pattern.

Chapter 11: Within-Subjects Designs

Key concepts

In a within-subjects (repeated-measures) design, every participant goes through every condition, serving as their own control. Individual differences — the biggest source of noise in between-subjects work — cancel out, so these designs are efficient and statistically powerful: fewer participants, cleaner comparisons.

The catch is order effects: experiencing one condition changes the next. Practice effects (getting better), fatigue effects (getting worse), and carryover effects (a drug, mood, or strategy bleeding across conditions) can all masquerade as the IV.

The fix is counterbalancing — varying condition order across participants so order effects average out. Complete counterbalancing uses every possible order (fine for 2–3 conditions); a Latin square gives each condition each position exactly once with far fewer orders; random order assigns a fresh random sequence per person.

Counterbalancing only handles order effects that are roughly equal across conditions. Differential carryover — where condition A ruins condition B but not vice versa — can’t be counterbalanced away. If the IV’s effect is permanent or irreversible (learning, surgery, trauma), use between-subjects instead.

Tip: Quizzes love the trade-off — within-subjects needs fewer people and controls individual differences, but order/carryover effects are the price.

Chapter 12: Within-Subjects Designs: Small N

Key concepts

Small-N designs study one participant (or a handful) intensively over time instead of averaging across a crowd. They’re the backbone of behavior analysis and clinical work, where the question is “did THIS person change?”

Everything starts with a baseline (A phase): measure the behavior repeatedly with no treatment until it’s stable. A drifting or bouncy baseline is useless — you can’t tell later whether the treatment did anything.

The ABAB (reversal) design is the workhorse: baseline → treatment → back to baseline → treatment again. If the behavior changes with the treatment, reverses when it’s withdrawn, and changes again when it’s reintroduced, that’s compelling causal evidence from a single person.

When withdrawing treatment would be unethical or the change is permanent, use a multiple-baseline design: start treatment at different times across behaviors, people, or settings. If each baseline holds steady until its own treatment starts, coincidence is ruled out.

The changing-criterion design raises the bar for reinforcement step by step (e.g., fewer cigarettes per week); the behavior should track each new criterion. Analysis is usually visual inspection of graphed data — looking for clear, immediate, large shifts at phase changes — rather than statistics.

Tip: ABAB = “did the behavior flip back when we pulled the treatment?” If yes, the treatment caused it.

Chapter 14: Drawing Conclusions

Key concepts

Statistical hypothesis testing runs on the logic of modus tollens: you can’t prove your research hypothesis, but you can disconfirm the null hypothesis (the claim that the IV had no effect). A significant result means the data would be very unlikely if the null were true — so you reject it.

The p value is that “how unlikely” number: the probability of getting results this extreme if the null were really true. If p falls below your alpha level (usually .05), you call the result statistically significant.

Two ways to be wrong: Type I error (false alarm — claiming an effect that isn’t there; probability = alpha) and Type II error (miss — failing to detect a real effect). They’re a seesaw: lowering alpha cuts false alarms but raises misses.

Statistical power is your chance of catching a real effect — boosted by bigger samples, bigger effects, and less noise. And significance isn’t size: effect size tells you how big the difference is, and a tiny effect can be “significant” with a huge sample.

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the true effect — more informative than a yes/no significance verdict. And statistical significance isn’t practical significance: always ask whether the effect matters in real life.

No single study settles anything. Replication checks that results hold up; generalization (external validity) asks how far they stretch beyond your sample, setting, and measures. The discussion section is where you interpret all of this honestly — what you found, what it means, what the limits are, and what’s next.

One more tool for the road: scales of measurement decide which statistics are legal. Nominal = categories, ordinal = ranks, interval = equal gaps with no true zero, ratio = equal gaps with a true zero. (Borrowed from Ch 13, which isn’t on this exam — but quizzes borrow from it.)

Chapter 15: Quasi-Experimental Designs

Chapter title inferred from the 9/28 lecture topic (“Discuss quasi-experiments”) — confirm against your class notes.

Key concepts

A quasi-experiment looks like an experiment — there’s an IV and a DV — but it’s missing the one ingredient that makes causality airtight: random assignment. Participants land in conditions through pre-existing groups (two classrooms, two clinics, before/after a policy change), so the groups may differ from the start.

The classic is the nonequivalent groups design: compare a treatment group with a similar-but-not-randomized control group. It beats having no comparison at all, but selection threats haunt every conclusion — maybe the groups differed before the IV ever showed up.

The interrupted time-series design takes many measurements before and after an event (a new law, a policy rollout). One “after” data point proves nothing, but a long stable baseline that shifts exactly at the interruption is persuasive — history threats are the main rival explanation.

Researchers patch the weaknesses with matching, statistical controls, and multiple pretests, but patches aren’t cures: without randomization, some alternative explanation always survives. That’s why quasi-experiments trade internal validity for realism — they’re how you study things you can’t ethically or practically randomize (laws, disasters, school policies).

Reading a quasi-experiment critically means asking one question on repeat: “What else differs between these groups besides the IV?”

Interrupted time-series design“Interrupt” L. interrumpere, to break between — rumpere (to break); “series” L. series, a chain.many observations before and after an event; looks for a shift at the interruption point.
Selection threat“Threat” OE threat, pressure or menace.pre-existing group differences that rival the IV as an explanation.
History threat“History” Gk. historia, an inquiry — from histor, one who knows.an outside event coinciding with the IV, offering an alternative explanation.
External validity“External” L. externus, outside — from exter, outer.how far results generalize to other people, settings, and times — often quasi-experiments’ strength.
Field experiment“Field” OE feld, open land — an experiment run out in the world.a true experiment run in a real-world setting — random assignment kept, lab control sacrificed.

Tip: If a question describes a study comparing two existing classrooms or a before/after policy change with no random assignment, the answer is quasi-experimental — and the threat is selection.

Chapter 16: Communicating Research: APA Style Manuscripts

Key concepts

Science isn’t finished until it’s communicated — other researchers can’t evaluate, replicate, or build on work they can’t read. That starts with the literature search: databases like PsycINFO, keyword strategies, and reading with an eye for what’s missing (the gap your study fills).

Only peer-reviewed sources count as real evidence; everything else is background noise. A literature review synthesizes what those sources say together — it doesn’t just list them one by one.

An APA manuscript follows a fixed recipe so readers always know where to look: title page, abstract (150–250 words condensing the whole study — question, method, results, conclusion), introduction (the funnel: broad topic → specific gap → your hypothesis), method (participants, materials/apparatus, procedure — detailed enough to replicate), results (what you found, no interpretation), discussion (what it means, limitations, next steps), references.

APA style rules that quizzes target: past tense for what you did and found, present tense for conclusions and established facts; every in-text citation must appear in the references and vice versa; numbers, headings, and tables follow strict formatting so results are unambiguous.

The same skeleton shrinks into conference abstracts and posters — and the abstract is often the only part anyone reads, so it has to stand alone.

Hardest Chapters

Every design chapter answers one question: how do I prove X caused Y? Each design is a different strategy for ruling out alternative explanations. Read this section when the chapter titles start blurring together.

Ch 8 — Between-Subjects Designs: Different people in each group: Group A gets the treatment, Group B doesn’t, you compare the groups. The whole thing only works if the groups were equivalent before the treatment — that’s what random assignment is for. This design exists because sometimes one person can’t do both conditions (you can’t un-learn something, and some treatments are permanent). Price: you need a lot of participants, and individual differences between groups become noise.

Ch 9 — Basic Between-Subjects Designs: The simplest versions of Ch 8: one variable, two or more groups. Posttest-only (measure once at the end), pretest-posttest (measure before and after — but the pretest can tip people off), Solomon four-group (runs both at once to check whether the pretest itself changed anything). These exist as the building blocks — learn the simple case before the fancy ones.

Ch 10 — Between-Subjects Factorial Designs: Testing two or more variables at the same time to see how they combine. A 2×2 crosses two factors (two levels each) into four groups. You get each factor’s main effect plus the interaction — whether one factor’s effect depends on the other’s level (spot it as non-parallel lines on a graph). Exists because real life never changes just one thing at a time, and the combination is often the interesting finding.

Ch 11 — Within-Subjects Designs: The same people do every condition — everyone is their own control, so individual differences cancel out. Fewer participants needed, cleaner comparisons. The danger is order effects: practice, fatigue, or carryover from one condition bleeding into the next. Fix it with counterbalancing (Latin square, random orders). But if the effect is permanent or carryover is one-directional, you must go back to between-subjects.

Ch 12 — Within-Subjects Designs: Small N: Studies with one person or a handful, tracked intensively over time — the backbone of clinical and behavior work, where the question is “did THIS person change?” Start with a stable baseline, then use ABAB (treatment on, off, on — if the behavior follows the treatment, that’s causal evidence) or multiple baselines when withdrawing treatment would be unethical. Exists because sometimes you only have one client and still need rigorous evidence.

Ch 14 — Drawing Conclusions: The “okay, but did it actually work?” chapter. You can’t prove your hypothesis; you can only reject the null (the claim of no effect). p < .05 means the result would be very unlikely if the null were true. Two ways to be wrong: Type I = false alarm, Type II = miss. Significance isn’t size — effect size tells you how big the difference is, and confidence intervals tell you the plausible range. Exists because data never speak for themselves; you need rules for what counts as evidence.

Ch 15 — Quasi-Experimental Designs: Looks like an experiment but you couldn’t randomly assign — you can’t randomly make someone a smoker or assign a school to a new policy. You compare pre-existing groups (nonequivalent groups) or many measurements before and after an event (interrupted time series) as carefully as you can. Weaker causal claims, because selection threats always survive. Exists because the real world won’t let you randomize everything, and a weaker answer beats no answer.

Ch 16 — Communicating Research: APA Style Manuscripts: How to write it all up in the standard format — abstract, introduction (funnel to your hypothesis), method (replication recipe), results (no interpretation), discussion (what it means, limits, next steps), references. Exists because science is a group project: findings don’t count until they’re communicated clearly enough for others to check and build on.

The thread: Chapters 8–12 are how you set up the comparison, Ch 14 is how you decide what the numbers mean, Ch 15 is what you do when the ideal setup is impossible, and Ch 16 is how you tell the world.

Commonly Confused Pairs

If you only memorize one page, make it this one. These are the distinctions quizzes are built to test.

  • Type I vs. Type II error: false alarm (claimed an effect that isn’t there) vs. miss (missed a real effect).
  • IV vs. DV: you manipulate the IV; you measure the DV.
  • Random assignment vs. random selection: assignment → which condition (internal validity); selection → who gets studied (generalizability).
  • Between-subjects vs. within-subjects: different people per condition vs. the same people in every condition.
  • Main effect vs. interaction: one factor’s overall effect vs. one factor’s effect depending on the other.
  • Statistical vs. practical significance: passed the p < .05 cutoff vs. actually matters in real life.
  • Confidentiality vs. anonymity: researcher could identify you but won’t vs. nobody can identify you.
  • Deception vs. debriefing: misleading during the study vs. explaining after it.
  • Hypothesis vs. theory: one testable prediction vs. the broad explanation behind it.
  • Basic vs. applied research: understanding for its own sake vs. solving a problem.
  • Internal vs. external validity: did the IV cause it (here) vs. does it generalize (elsewhere).
  • Null vs. research hypothesis: “no effect” (what you test against) vs. “there IS an effect” (what you hope).
  • Complete vs. partial counterbalancing: every possible order vs. a subset like a Latin square.
  • Posttest-only vs. pretest-posttest: no pretest (no testing threat) vs. pretest (checks equivalence, risks sensitization).
  • True experiment vs. quasi-experiment: random assignment present vs. absent — the whole causal difference.
